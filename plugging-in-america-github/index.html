The grid would need about a third more electricity

Question: if all ~290 million cars plus every truck and bus were electric, how much extra electricity would the US use in a year?

US electricity per year, in terawatt-hours (TWh)

Grey bar = everything the US generated in 2025. Colored bar = what an all-electric road fleet would add, split by vehicle type. 1 TWh = 1 billion kWh, about what 90,000 homes use in a year.

Sources: EIA (2025 generation, 4,430 TWh); FHWA Highway Statistics VM-1 (≈3.3 trillion vehicle-miles). Efficiency assumptions in Methods.

Formula: miles driven × kWh per mile (from the wall plug, including charging losses). Cars and pickups are about 60% of the new demand, and big rigs are about a quarter.

The problem isn't how much energy. It's when people charge.

Question: what would the busiest hour look like if everyone plugged in when they got home, versus if charging were scheduled for the quiet hours?

Power demand across a hot summer day, lower-48 states (gigawatts, GW)

Hover to read each hour. Grey = today's demand on a record day (peak ≈ 759 GW, July 2025). The two colored lines carry the same ~4.4 TWh/day of charging, delivered on two different schedules.

Illustrative model: today's curve is a typical summer peak-day shape scaled to EIA's 2025 record. "Naive" assumes most drivers plug in on arriving home (5–9 pm). "Managed" fills the overnight valley, keeping a 40 GW floor for charging that can't wait.

The same cars and the same energy give two very different grids. Unmanaged charging pushes the peak to about 1,170 GW (+54%), which means hundreds of new power plants. Managed charging tops out near 820 GW (+8%), because the cars charge in the overnight valley when the grid has spare capacity.

Which clean power source could supply it?

Question: what would it cost if solar, wind, hydro, geothermal or nuclear supplied all of it, counting when each one actually produces power?

Cost to build enough of each source to supply ~1,600 TWh/yr (trillions of US dollars)

Each bar spans low to high estimates and includes the batteries each source needs to cover nighttime charging. Hydro can't scale: the US has few dam sites left, so it could supply only ~4% of the need.

Sources: Lazard LCOE+ 2025 (unsubsidized cost ranges); NREL Annual Technology Baseline (battery costs, ~$250–350/kWh). Capacity factors: solar 25%, wind 36%, geothermal 90%, nuclear 92%.

☀️ Solar: produces at middayCheapest panels, but most cars charge at night, so about 60% of the output has to be stored in batteries first.
💨 Wind: stronger at nightA good match for overnight charging. The best wind is on the Great Plains, so it needs long transmission lines.
⚛️🌋 Nuclear and geothermal: steady 24/7Ideal for overnight charging, but slow (nuclear) or unproven at scale (enhanced geothermal).
A mix beats any single source: ~40% wind, 35% solar, 10% geothermal, 10% nuclear, 5% hydro, plus batteries comes to about $1.1–1.7 trillion, less than any one source alone, because each one fills the others' gaps.

The full bill: $3–5 trillion, which fuel savings repay in about a decade

Question: on top of power plants, what do the batteries, chargers, neighborhood wiring and high-voltage power lines cost?

One-time build-out cost (trillions of $)

Low and high estimates, stacked by component.

What the country pays for vehicle energy each year (billions of $)

Today's gasoline and diesel vs. the electricity that would replace it.

Gasoline ≈135 billion gal × ~$3.20; on-road diesel ≈45 billion gal × ~$3.70 (EIA). Electricity: 1,600 TWh × $0.10–0.16/kWh.

Chargers: NREL's 2030 National Charging Network, scaled from 33 million to ~290 million vehicles. Transmission: ~50–80k miles at $3–6M per mile. Local wiring: ~$1,000–2,500 per vehicle with managed charging. The cost of the vehicles themselves is not included.

Drivers would spend roughly $350–450 billion a year less on energy, so the infrastructure pays for itself in about 10 years. Without managed charging, the bill rises toward $5–6 trillion.

How long would it take? 20–30 years, held up mainly by power lines

Question: how long would the build-out take, and what's the hardest part?

How long one project takes, start to finish (years)

Bars show the typical range. The dashed line is how long the vehicle fleet takes to replace itself even if 100% of new sales were electric starting today (~20 years).

Sources: Lawrence Berkeley National Lab "Queued Up" (≈5-year median wait to connect a new plant to the grid); utility transformer lead-time reports (2–4 years); FERC Order 1920; project histories (Vogtle, SunZia, Grain Belt Express).

The hardest part is high-voltage transmission. The plan needs ~50,000–80,000 miles of new lines. Recently the US has built only a few hundred miles a year of its largest lines, and each one needs approval from multiple states. Fastest fix: replacing the wires on existing towers with advanced conductors can roughly double a line's capacity in 1–3 years.

The carbon payoff: about one-fifth of US emissions

Question: how much carbon would this cut, compared with other megaprojects that cost a similar amount?

US greenhouse emissions, billion tonnes CO₂-equivalent per year

Gross emissions ≈ 6.3 Gt (EPA Inventory). The highlighted slice is what electrifying road vehicles removes (clean grid case).

Carbon avoided per $1 trillion spent (billion tonnes, over 30 years)

Same price tag, very different climate return.

Tailpipe emissions: EPA Inventory of US Greenhouse Gas Emissions (cars and light trucks ≈1.05 Gt, trucks and buses ≈0.48 Gt). Upstream oil emissions +0.15–0.3 Gt. Battery manufacturing ≈−0.13 Gt/yr spread over vehicle life. Megaproject costs in today's dollars (Brown Univ. Costs of War; CBO; FHWA).

Net cut: ~0.9 Gt/yr on today's grid, ~1.5 Gt/yr on a clean grid. Spending the same money on direct air capture machines would remove roughly 5× less carbon. The wars in Iraq and Afghanistan and the COVID relief packages cost as much or more, with no climate benefit.

So why do mostly upper-middle-class households buy EVs?

Question: what are the biggest hurdles to mass adoption, and why is it concentrated among the upper middle class?

Fuel cost to drive one mile (US cents)

The cheap fuel is only cheap if you can charge at home. Renters and apartment dwellers often can't.

EV at 0.33 kWh/mi. Home power $0.17/kWh (EIA avg. residential); public fast charging ~$0.50/kWh; gas car 28 mpg at $3.20/gal.

The filters: each one removes more households

An upper-middle-class household tends to pass all of them at once.

💵 PriceNew EVs average ~$55–58k, and there are few models under $35k.
🏠 Home chargingRequires a driveway or garage; ~35% of households rent.
🧾 IncentivesThe $7,500 federal credit ended Sept 30, 2025, and most of it went to higher earners.
🚙 Used market2 of 3 cars sold are used, but used EVs are still scarce.
🛡️ Insurance and credit~20–30% higher insurance; worse credit means higher interest on a pricier car.
🌾 Rural use and coldLong drives, towing, few chargers; winter cuts range 20–40%.
"It takes money to save money." Lower-income households spend the largest share of their income on gas, so they would gain the most, yet they face the most barriers. Cheaper models, a used-EV market with battery-health certification, curbside chargers, "right-to-charge" laws for renters, and income-targeted rebates at the point of sale would widen access.

Sources: Kelley Blue Book / Cox Automotive (2025–26 prices and sales); Borenstein & Davis, "The Distributional Effects of US Clean Energy Tax Credits" (NBER); Census American Housing Survey (renters).

Winter is the real test, not summer

Question: how does the picture change in winter, when EVs use ~30% more energy and heat pumps raise demand?

Power demand across a cold winter weekday, lower-48 states (GW)

Hover to read each hour. Winter demand has two humps (morning and evening) and only a shallow dip overnight, because heating runs all night. Charging rises to ~5.8 TWh/day (+30% for cold batteries and cabin heat).

Illustrative model: today's curve is a typical cold-day shape scaled to the US winter record (~745 GW). The heat-pump layer assumes moderate building electrification (+85 to +180 GW depending on the hour). The EV charging profile is the summer one × 1.3.

Peak demand by season and charging style (GW)

Same EV fleet, three conditions. The dashed line is today's all-time record (759 GW).

Winter has no quiet hours left to fill. Scheduled charging fills every gap, and demand flattens out at about 1,030 GW around the clock. That's the lowest scheduling can go, and it's ~210 GW above the summer case. With winter solar at about half its summer output, the gap has to be filled by wind, nuclear, geothermal and hydro: roughly $0.3–0.8 trillion more. The biggest risk is a multi-day cold, calm snap, like Winter Storms Uri (2021) and Elliott (2022).

What if parked cars powered the grid?

Question: if vehicle-to-grid (V2G) lets parked cars (~20 TWh of batteries) send power back, how much grid battery storage disappears?

Grid storage needed vs. what parked cars could offer (TWh)

Only a slice of the fleet's 20 TWh is usable: ~70% of cars plugged in at once, owners share 20–30% of their battery, and 10–50% of owners join.

Cost: grid batteries vs. V2G hardware (billions of $)

For the mixed-portfolio plan (~1 TWh of storage). V2G needs two-way home chargers (~$1,000–3,000 extra each, ~60M homes).

Payments to car owners (likely a few hundred $/yr each) and battery wear are extra, so the net saving is about $100–250B.

✅ Replaces daily batteriesThe middle scenario (~1 TWh, ~430 GW) covers all the grid batteries in the mixed plan, and about 40% of the solar-heavy plan.
✅ Covers the evening peak~430 GW of power that can be sent back on demand is more than the evening peak needs, so many gas "peaker" plants are no longer needed.
❌ Can't fix multi-day cold snapsV2G only moves energy within a day. On a flat winter day there's no low point to recharge from, and drivers need their range.
Cars can replace short-duration grid batteries, not long-lasting power plants. V2G could save roughly $100–250 billion and pay EV owners. But the winter problem from section 9, too little energy over several cold days, still needs plants that can run on demand or long-duration storage.

How this was made, and how to redo or extend it

What I asked the AI to do

This exhibit came out of a conversation with an AI assistant (Claude, by Anthropic). Each question below led to one calculation. The AI ran web searches for current figures, used estimates from published studies for the rest, and wrote the Python arithmetic for the charging model.

  1. "If the USA made all automobiles electric, calculate added annual electricity, peak load under naive charging, and peak load under managed charging, beside present generation and peak demand." → Miles driven by vehicle class (FHWA) × kWh/mile → annual TWh. Peak: 290M vehicles × ~50% plug in × ~40% charging at once × ~6 kW, plus ~100 GW of trucks. Managed: spread daily energy across the overnight valley in the hourly demand curve (chart 2).
  2. "Cost of supplying it with solar, wind, hydro, geothermal and nuclear, factoring in time of day, plus batteries, chargers and power lines." → Capacity = TWh ÷ (8,760 h × capacity factor); build cost = capacity × $/W; storage sized from the share of energy needed at night; charger, wiring and transmission costs scaled up from NREL and utility studies.
  3. "How long would the build-out take, and what's the hardest part?" → Lead times compared with fleet turnover (~16M new vehicles/yr, ~12.5-year average age).
  4. "How much carbon would it cut, compared with similar megaprojects?" → Tailpipe + upstream emissions minus charging-electricity and battery-manufacturing emissions. Tonnes ÷ $ over a 30-year asset life.
  5. "List every net-zero project, rank by $/tonne, and place EVs." → 24 projects with a midpoint cost and cut size, sorted into a cost curve (chart 7).
  6. "Biggest hurdles to mass EV adoption and why mostly upper-middle-class buyers." → Fuel cost per mile, plus research on prices, tax-credit distribution and housing.
  7. "Turn this discussion into a colorful webpage with graphs." → This page: plain HTML and hand-drawn SVG charts, no build step.
  8. "How does the picture change in winter, when EVs use ~30% more energy and heat pumps raise demand?" → Cold-day demand shape scaled to the ~745 GW winter record + a heat-pump layer + EV charging × 1.3. Managed charging is re-solved by filling the gaps in daily demand (chart 9).
  9. "What if vehicle-to-grid lets parked cars send power back? How much grid storage disappears?" → Usable storage = 20 TWh × share of owners participating × 70% plugged in × share of battery owners allow (20–30%); power = participating cars × ~7 kW. Compared with the grid battery needs from section 3 (chart 10).

Limitations

These are order-of-magnitude estimates, not forecasts. The summer and winter hourly curves are stylized shapes, not measured grid data. The heat-pump layer and V2G participation rates are assumptions. Costs vary a lot by region, interest rates, tariffs and permitting. Several costs come from AI summaries of published studies rather than a full literature review. Check the primary sources before relying on any single number.
